About me

I began my career in Indian classrooms, first as an elementary teacher and then as a special educator, and quickly learned that even the most devoted teacher cannot out-teach a system that wasn't designed for all of its learners. That realization has shaped my path in education.

I am currently a Postdoctoral Fellow in Educational Leadership and Policy at The University of Texas at Austin, where I study how school leaders build intervention structures for students with disabilities. Alongside the fellowship, I consult for Pratham Education Foundation's ENABLE project and serve on a Department of School Education & Literacy, Ministry of Education working committee developing a national teacher training module on inclusive education that adopts a tiered approach to intervention.

I hold a Ph.D. in Curriculum and Instruction, with a specialization in Educational Leadership, from Boston College's Lynch School of Education and Human Development. My dissertation identified the system-level conditions of moral authorization and organizational capacity that make inclusion last. Before that, I partnered with Boston Public Schools on special education reform and led assessment research at Pratham's ASER Centre, including the Assessment for All tool for children with disabilities.

Throughout this work runs one consistent question: how do education systems, not just individual classrooms, become genuinely inclusive, and stay that way?

Teaching

A decade of teaching across two continents, from elementary classrooms in India to graduate seminars in Boston.

Teaching philosophy

I teach with one lesson from my own classroom years in view: good intentions collapse quickly when teachers lack time, usable strategies, and coordinated support. Most of my students are practicing educators and emerging leaders who need something that works on Monday morning. So I teach the way I believe professional judgment is built, close to practice, oriented toward improvement, and disciplined by evidence. Each unit opens with a real problem of practice, such as a co-teaching model launched without the staffing to sustain it, or a school whose inclusive enrollment outpaced its capacity. We then work backward to the theory, the evidence, and the method, so that students learn to look past whether a practice worked and ask why it worked, for which learners, and in what settings.

My approach is deliberately eclectic. It is student-centered, because adults learn best when the curriculum grows from the problems they already carry; it is constructivist, treating the classroom as an exchange where my research meets the authority of my students' practice; and it is rigorous about the core knowledge every leader must master, from evidence use and assessment to systems thinking. Whether in person or online, I design courses in which working educators feel individually supported, and I choose cases that reflect the real intersections of disability, language, and culture. Above all, I want students to leave able to read a learner, read a system, and act to improve both.
